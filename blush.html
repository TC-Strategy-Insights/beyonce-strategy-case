The problem

Tutorials assume you already know the words.

Makeup tutorials are everywhere, and that is the problem. They sit on different platforms, assume vocabulary a beginner has not learned, and rarely say how long a look takes or what skill it needs. People gave up before they started.

What people wanted
Tutorials in one place, matched to what I can actually do today.
What I set out to build
A site that sorts tutorials by skill level and by the time you have.

Who I designed for

Two users, opposite ends of the same problem.

Interviews and empathy maps surfaced a split that shaped everything after it. Beginners and experts were both underserved, for opposite reasons.

Kira
19 · college student · beginner
“I know relatively nothing about makeup.”
Needs plain language, short tutorials, and to know which brush does what. Jargon and advanced technique send her away.
Andrea
34 · data analyst · advanced
“I am looking for a tutorial that will help me serve face.”
Needs a challenge. Basic tutorials bore her, and she cannot tell in advance whether a look will suit her complexion.

Usability testing

What testing actually found.

I ran usability sessions on the prototype and grouped every observation on an affinity board. Four themes emerged. The first one appeared in three separate sessions, which made it the priority.

01

People could not find tutorials at their level

The core promise of the product was the thing users could not locate. Three separate participants went looking for skill-based tutorials and failed. One bypassed the level system entirely and used a plain tutorial link instead.

3 of 5 participants
“Participant didn't know where to find makeup tutorial based on MU knowledge base.” “I do think that it should be more options for level up tab for tutorials 5–7.”
02

The words on screen were not the words users had

A participant could not complete a task because the site said “brow” and she was looking for “eyebrow.” Another questioned whether “MUA” would read to anyone outside the industry. Vocabulary was quietly blocking navigation.

Task failure
“When prompted participant was unable to locate the word eyebrow, did not understand verbiage 'brow'.” “Participant is frustrated that labels don't feel more intuitive to use.”
03

Basic navigation was missing

No home key. A search icon people tried and could not use. No clear signup path, so participants could not tell what the site wanted them to do, or who it was for.

Structural
“You don't have a home key.” “Unable to use search icon.” “I don't have any calls for action… I don't know if it's a platform for MUA to join?”
04

Static images where people expected video

Users assumed a tutorial meant video. Still images read as instructions they would have to interpret alone, which is exactly the gap the product existed to close.

Content format
“For the tutorials should have videos not pictures. If you want a tutorial it should have a mini video.” “Tutorials was not enough — felt the website did not meet my needs.”

What changed

Findings, turned into revisions.

“Brow”
“Eyebrow”
Site-wide language rewrite to the words users said out loud, not industry shorthand. The vocabulary failure was a task blocker, not a preference.
Level Up as one page
Skill tier surfaced on every tutorial
If three of five people cannot find the level system, the level system is in the wrong place. Skill became a visible filter, not a destination.
No home key, dead search
Persistent nav with working search
Users need a way back and a way to look. Both were assumed and neither existed in the tested build.
Still images
Video-first tutorials, filterable by time
Users asked for “five minute face” options chosen by time available, not just by skill. Time became a filter alongside level.

Accessibility ran through the build rather than sitting at the end: alt text on every image for screen readers, icons paired with labels so navigation did not depend on symbol literacy, and the plain-language rewrite that came directly out of testing.

What I took from it

The first idea is a hypothesis, not a design.

The strongest finding in this project was the one I would not have predicted: a single word, “brow,” stopped someone from finishing a task. Nothing in the wireframes would have caught that. It only surfaced because I put the prototype in front of people and watched where they stopped.

Given another round, I would retest the revised language and level filters with the same participants, and look specifically at whether the site reads as a place for learners, for artists, or both. That ambiguity came up in testing and I have not resolved it.

Findings quoted above are verbatim participant observations from the usability sessions, recorded on the project affinity board. Participant names are withheld. Blush is a concept product; the research, testing, and design decisions are my own.
